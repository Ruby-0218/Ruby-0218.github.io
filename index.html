Hi, I’m Ju-Ting Sun, a Master’s student in the Quantitative Methods in the Social Sciences (Data Science Track) program at Columbia University. I’m interested in using data to make sense of real-world problems, especially around climate, energy, policy, and technology. My path here has been a little unconventional. I started out studying Political Science, but over time I became more curious about what happens after we identify a problem: How do we measure it? How do we know whether a solution actually works? And can we build something useful around it? That curiosity eventually pulled me toward quantitative methods and data science.

A big part of that journey has been Carbon Walker, a climate-tech startup I co-founded in Taiwan. We built an automated transportation carbon-tracking platform that helps individuals and companies turn everyday mobility into measurable emissions data, with applications around standards such as ISO 14064 and CBAM. Building a startup has taught me a lot beyond the technical side — a good idea still has to make sense for real users, regulations, business needs, and sometimes very unpredictable human behavior. And yes, I’ve also learned that starting a company is definitely harder than it looks from the outside.

At Columbia, I’ve been able to explore these questions from a few different angles. I’ve worked on sustainability and greenwashing analysis, used NLP to study changes in BlackRock’s corporate language, and led a technical project with IBM to improve the workflow and usability of a generative AI product. I tend to enjoy projects where the data starts out messy, the question is not perfectly defined, and there is something useful to build or learn at the end.

Most recently, I interned at NociQuant, where I worked on turning fragmented longitudinal clinical narratives into structured findings and helped design a framework for representing how information changes over time. Healthcare was a completely new domain for me, but I liked the underlying problem: taking complicated, unstructured information and figuring out how to make it more consistent, measurable, and useful.

Before Columbia, I studied Political Science at National Cheng Kung University. I worked on policy research, conducted qualitative interviews with local political actors, and once built a fair-trade platform aimed at helping small farmers. Looking back, those experiences probably explain why I’m still drawn to problems involving sustainability, policy, markets, and human behavior — just now with a few more quantitative tools in the toolbox.

These days, I’m especially curious about climate and energy problems where better data can lead to better decisions. I’m still exploring where exactly that takes me, but I tend to gravitate toward questions with a practical “so what?” — why some renewable projects get built while others don’t, whether sustainability claims match what happens in reality, or how limited energy and infrastructure can be used more efficiently.

Outside of work, I’m a huge sports fan. I’ve followed tennis Grand Slams since fifth grade and have probably spent way too many hours stressing over Novak Djokovic’s matches. If you’re curious why I think he has a pretty convincing GOAT case, I wrote a whole page about it here: The GOAT. I also recently started watching F1. I first noticed Lewis Hamilton mostly because his name reminded me of the Broadway musical “Hamilton” (one of my favorite shows), but his driving quickly gave me a much better reason to keep watching.

And finally, I’m proud of my music library. Most of the singers and bands I like are either old, retired, or already disbanded, so my taste might be a little cursed. Curious whether it’s actually cursed or secretly elite? Take a look at my music blog and judge for yourself.
